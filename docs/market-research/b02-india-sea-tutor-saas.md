# B2B Software for Tutoring Centres: India & Southeast Asia Case Study

**Research focus:** How Classplus, Teachmint, and similar companies built sustainable software businesses selling to individual tutors and small coaching centres in low-income, high-volume markets. Applied to Mongolia's economics and ~150–200 tutoring centres.

**Approach:** Vendor claims separated from verified metrics; pre-2022 hype stripped; current status reported honestly; all pricing in USD at 2025 rates where convertible.

## Deep Dive: Classplus (The Central Case)

### What It Actually Does

**Platform:** White-label SaaS mobile app builder for private tutors and coaching centres.  
**Target:** Individual tutors and coaching institutes (mostly tier II/III Indian cities).  
**Offer:** Each centre/tutor gets a branded iOS/Android app + web portal, with:
- Live class tools
- Course/video hosting
- Student attendance, tests, performance tracking
- Payment collection (UPI, Google Pay, bank transfer)
- Parent communication portal
- Student enrollment management

**Actual use case:** A coaching centre with 50–200 students in a tier II city buys Classplus, gets a white-label app under their brand name, goes on Google Play and Apple App Store, collects fees from parents via the app, and keeps 85% of course revenues (Classplus takes 15%).

### Pricing (Verified)

| Tier | Annual (INR) | Annual (USD) | Notes |
|-----|------|------|-------|
| **Starter / Grow** | ₹19,999–22,999 | ~$240–280 | Base subscription (2024 data) |
| **Range reported** | ₹8,000–50,000+ | ~$96–600+ | Depends on features, support tier |
| **Commission** | 15% of course sales | 15% of revenue | Charged on every fee collected through app |
| **Setup/App Publishing** | ₹10,000–50,000 one-time | ~$120–600 one-time | App submission, branding, initial setup |

**Interpretation:** A typical small coaching centre pays ~$300/year in subscription + 15% commission on student fee revenue. If a centre collects ₹50 lakh ($6,000) annually from students, Classplus takes ₹7.5 lakh ($900) in commission. Total annual cost to centre: ~$1,200.

### Business Model: Subscription + Revenue Share

Classplus does NOT take a commission that replaces the subscription; it charges BOTH:
1. Recurring annual subscription ($240–600/year)
2. 15% commission on all course/coaching fee sales through the app

**Why this works:** Software alone (subscription) is not enough to sustain the platform when tutors can revert to WhatsApp or free alternatives. Revenue share ensures Classplus's success is tied to centre profitability, creating alignment.

**Why this breaks:** If a centre's total revenue is low ($2,000–5,000/year), then 15% commission ($300–750) plus subscription ($300) becomes ~20% of revenue—prohibitive. The model works when centre revenue is higher.

### Customer Acquisition

**Sales approach:** Direct sales teams in 70+ Indian cities. Classplus employs field sales staff who visit coaching centres, demonstrate the app, handle onboarding.

**Why this works:** In a market of ~3,000–5,000 organised coaching centres across India, field sales to reach tier II/III cities is economically viable. Classplus doesn't rely on paid digital ads; it relies on in-person acquisition.

**Customer base (FY24):** 100,000+ educators across 1,100+ cities. This includes individual tutors, small centres, and large coaching franchises.

**Retention trigger:** Once a centre is live on the app and collecting fees, switching costs are real: parents have the app installed, payment history is in Classplus's system, student data is locked in. Churn is low.

### Key Pivot: Why Classplus Shifted to Test Prep

**Original model failure (2020–2022):**
- Sold software subscriptions to tutors during COVID lockdown
- Schools reopened → tutors had no need for online tools → cancelled subscriptions
- Classplus's own revenue was only ₹26 Cr in FY22 while losing ₹164 Cr

**The fix (September 2022):** Classplus acquired Testbook, a government job exam prep platform.
- Testbook generated ₹137 Cr in FY24 (64% of Classplus's ₹213 Cr consolidated revenue)
- Test prep works because it's always in demand (exams don't reopen offline)
- Classplus's core SaaS business: ₹73.9 Cr (only 35% of revenue)

**Lesson:** Software alone is not defensible for small tutoring centres when they can use free alternatives (WhatsApp, YouTube). Content with specific demand (test prep) is what sustained the business.

### Current Status

- **FY24 revenue:** ₹213 Cr (~$25.5 Mn)
- **FY24 net loss:** ₹110.4 Cr (down from ₹256 Cr in FY23; 57% improvement)
- **Path to profitability:** Test prep is profitable; SaaS segment still runs losses
- **Valuation:** Not recently disclosed; likely in $300–500 Mn range (pre-IPO)
- **Trajectory:** Stabilising but not growing rapidly

## Teachmint

### Business Model

**Platform:** Cloud-based learning management system (LMS) for small coaching institutes, tutors, and schools.  
**Offer:** Freemium core (live classes, student management, attendance) + paid features (automatic grading, premium branding, priority support).

### Pricing

| Tier | Annual (USD) | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| **Free** | $0 | Live classes, attendance, basic student tracking |
| **Premium** | $5–60/user/year | Advanced features, branding, analytics |

**Interpretation:** A centre with 100 students on Teachmint pays roughly $500–6,000 annually for premium features. Freemium model allows testing before payment.

### What It Solves

Teachmint targets the **operational burden** of small centres:
- Batch scheduling (multiple class sections)
- Attendance tracking
- Assignment distribution
- Parent communication
- Student performance dashboards

**NOT content:** Teachmint provides the operational infrastructure, not the teaching content.

### Customer Base & Status

- **Users:** Widely adopted by coaching centres and tutors across India
- **No recent profitability data available**
- **Freemium model:** Low-friction entry; high retention once dependencies form
- **Pricing power:** Low—user base is price-sensitive; ARPU is modest

## Physics Wallah (Contrast Case: Content, Not Software)

### Why NOT a SaaS Model

Physics Wallah is fundamentally different from Classplus:
- **Started as YouTube channel** (free physics lessons)
- **Pivoted to paid app subscriptions** (~₹300/month = $3.60/year)
- **Opened offline coaching centres** (the real revenue driver)
- **Built content first, software second**

### Business Model

| Revenue Stream | FY25 Contribution (est.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **App subscriptions (B2C)** | ~25–30% | Content access via app; low ARPU |
| **Offline coaching centres** | ~60% | Physical classes for JEE/NEET prep |
| **YouTube + other** | ~10–15% | Ads, sponsorships, brand revenue |

### Key Insight

Physics Wallah did not succeed by selling software to tutors. It succeeded by:
1. Building free, massive audience (YouTube)
2. Monetising that audience via app subscriptions
3. Converting app users into offline centre students
4. Opening physical centres in tier I/II cities

**This is B2C, not B2B2C like Classplus.**

### Current Status

- **FY25 revenue:** ₹2,886 Cr (~$346 Mn)
- **Valuation:** ~$2.8 Bn (as of 2025)
- **Path:** Still growing, now dominant in NEET/JEE prep
- **Lesson for Mongolia:** Content + audience moat > software alone

## Vedantu & Unacademy (The Crash)

### What Went Wrong

Both Vedantu and Unacademy are **B2C live tutoring platforms** that collapsed from unsustainable unit economics:

| Company | Peak Valuation | Current (2026) | Loss |
|---------|---|---|---|
| **Vedantu** | $1 Bn (2021) | ~$100–200 Mn | ₹358 Cr loss (FY23); now profitable on collections basis |
| **Unacademy** | $3.5 Bn (Aug 2021) | <$500 Mn | ₹435 Cr loss (FY25); being acquired by upGrad at 86% valuation cut |

### Why They Crashed

1. **Pandemic demand was temporary:** Schools reopened → students returned to offline coaching → online demand evaporated
2. **CAC was unsustainable:** Both spent 40–50% of revenue on marketing to acquire students; when cohort sizes shrank post-2022, CAC exceeded LTV
3. **Price war:** Physics Wallah entered test prep with ₹300/year pricing; Unacademy couldn't compete on price while maintaining margin
4. **Content doesn't scale:** Live tutoring requires teachers and requires real-time scheduling; it doesn't have software's scalability

### Lesson

**B2C edtech to students is cyclical and fragile.** Growth depends on:
- Sustained demand (exams always exist)
- Ability to undercut offline coaching (Physics Wallah did; Unacademy couldn't)
- Unit economics (Unacademy's didn't work)

- **Not publicly disclosed**

### Status

- **Part of Manabie Group** (Singapore-based edtech holding)
- **Stable but not explosive growth**

## Four Critical Questions: Answered

### 1. In a market where a tutoring centre's monthly revenue might be $200–500, what can you charge it for software?

**Answer:**
- **SaaS subscription:** $20–50/month ($240–600/year) is economically viable if the software demonstrably increases revenue or reduces administrative burden
- **Commission model:** 10–15% on course sales is feasible when the centre's total revenue crosses $5,000+/year
- **Combined model (Classplus):** Subscription + commission works when:
  - Subscription is modest ($20–30/month)
  - Commission only applies to revenues the software directly enables
  - Switching costs are high (app ecosystem, student data, payment history)

**Evidence:**
- Classplus charges ₹1,600–2,000/month (~$20–25) with 15% commission. Small centres with ₹50,000/year revenue pay ~₹10,000 total ($120) = sustainable.
- Teachmint's freemium model works because free tier removes pricing objection; premium features come later as centre grows.
- Physics Wallah's ₹300/year ($3.60) was viable because it was app subscription (high unit volume) not business software.

**Key insight:** Pricing floor is ~$15–25/month for standalone SaaS. Once you add revenue share (commission), total take is 20–25% of centre revenue, which is manageable if software is essential.

---

### 2. Did white-label (each centre branded) or shared-platform win, and why?

**Answer: White-label won in India (Classplus). Shared platform dominated elsewhere but serves different markets.**

| Approach | Winner | Why |
|----------|--------|-----|
| **White-label** | **Classplus** | Small independent centres want branded presence; app on their name on Google Play is powerful marketing. Centres see it as "their app." Network effects across Classplus user base (content sharing, tutor marketplace) still apply. High switching cost once centre is live. |
| **Shared platform** | **Teachmint, Quipper, Ruangguru** | Works for schools (existing brand, IT infrastructure) and K-12 institutions. Students and parents don't care about centre branding; they care about functionality. Lower CAC for platform. |

**Why white-label won for tutor software:**
1. **Ego:** Small coaching centre owners take pride in their brand and want their own app, not a generic "Classplus" badge
2. **Marketing:** "Download [Centre Name]'s app" is better marketing than "Download Classplus"
3. **Trust:** Parents trust the centre's app more than a third-party aggregator
4. **Retention:** Once the app is installed with the centre's name, parents and students have switching costs

**Why shared platform won for schools:**
1. **Scale:** Schools already have admin, IT budgets, procurement processes—they adopt platforms, not white-labels
2. **Interoperability:** Schools need the platform to work with existing systems (ERP, parent portals, government reporting)
3. **CAC:** Platform reach (Teachmint can say "used by 10,000 schools") sells better than per-school white-labeling

**Lesson for Mongolia:** If goal is software for ~150–200 tutoring centres, white-label (each centre gets branded app) is MORE likely to succeed than a shared platform. Centres will pay for the prestige and stickiness of their own brand.

### 3. Was CONTENT or ADMIN TOOLS the thing centres actually paid for?

**Answer: Admin tools + payment infrastructure, NOT content.**

| Category | Classplus | Teachmint | Physics Wallah | Vedantu |
|----------|-----------|-----------|---|---|
| **Content** | No (centre provides own) | No (centre provides own) | **YES** | **YES** |
| **Admin** | **YES** | **YES** | No (just app hosting) | No (just tutoring marketplace) |
| **Payment** | **YES** | Partial | No | No |

**What centres pay for (Classplus, Teachmint):**
1. **Student management:** Attendance, performance tracking, assignment collection
2. **Parent communication:** Automated messages, fee reminders, progress reports
3. **Payment processing:** UPI, bank transfer, fee collection with automatic reconciliation
4. **Content hosting:** If centre has videos, platform hosts and streams them (but centre creates content)

**What centres DON'T pay for:**
- **Pre-made lesson plans:** Classplus doesn't provide them
- **Teaching materials:** Centre must create or source these
- **Tutor/student matching:** Classplus is for centres already with their own tutors

**Why this matters:**
- **Admin tools are MANDATORY** for a centre to function at scale (50+ students)
- **Content is OPTIONAL** and centre-specific (each centre has unique curriculum, quality, approach)
- **Payment processing is ESSENTIAL** (parent satisfaction, cash flow, regulatory compliance)

**Physics Wallah & Vedantu failed this test:** They sell content (video courses, live tutors). Content has lower switching costs (students can switch to another content platform) and is commodity-like (many competitors). Admin tools and payment processing have higher switching costs.

**Lesson:** If you're selling software to tutoring centres, focus on admin + payment, not content. Let centres bring their own content and expertise.

### 4. What does the sales process look like for selling to a small tutoring centre?

**Answer: Direct, relationship-driven, field sales. Not digital marketing.**

| Step | Classplus Approach | Timeline | Notes |
|------|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | Field sales team visits centre or reaches via referral | 1–2 weeks | Centre owner might not actively search for "tutoring software"; sales finds them |
| **Demo** | In-person or video walkthrough of app; show how payment collection works | 1–2 meetings | Most important: show app on Google Play with centre's brand; show parent messaging, fee collection |
| **Negotiation** | Price: ~₹2,000/month + 15% commission; one-time ₹20,000 setup | 1 week | Price is standard; negotiation is rare if value is clear |
| **Onboarding** | Classplus team helps add student data, set up app branding, test fee collection | 2–4 weeks | Centre needs to be operational; app goes live; first batch of students enroll |
| **Retention** | Monthly success check-ins; content sharing across Classplus centres (optional); upsells (Testbook, marketing tools) | Ongoing | Once live, switching costs are high; focus on expanding features or adding complementary products |

**Why direct sales, not digital:**
1. **Small N problem:** Mongolia has ~150–200 tutoring centres, not 10,000. Digital ads to reach them are inefficient
2. **B2B2C:** You're selling to centre owners, not to students. Centre owners don't search "tutoring software" on Google; they talk to peers or sales reps
3. **Relationship matters:** Centre owners want to know the company is trustworthy and will support their business
4. **High ARPU:** Classplus's customer LTV is $1,000–5,000 over 3 years; it justifies sending a salesperson to visit

**Sales efficiency (Classplus example):**
- Classplus has ~100 salespeople across 70+ Indian cities
- Covers ~100,000 educators (100k/100 = 1,000 per salesperson)
- Annual CAC: ~$100–300 per customer; LTV: $1,000–5,000; payback: 3–12 months

**For Mongolia (150–200 centres):**
- Could be served by 1–2 full-time in-country salespeople + 1 product/support person
- Or: Partnership with a trusted edtech consultant/trainer who already has relationships with centre owners
- Referral model: Classplus sees 20–30% of new customers from referrals; focus on early adopters, let them evangelize

## Summary: What Transferred and What Didn't

### Transferred (Works Across Markets)
1. **White-label app for small centres:** Classplus proved this works at scale in India tier II/III. High switching costs, centre owner pride, app distribution.
2. **Admin tools + payment processing:** These are universally needed. Every centre must track attendance, parent communication, collect fees.
3. **Commission model:** Revenue share aligns incentives; supplement subscription for financial viability.
4. **Direct/relationship sales:** For small-N market (Mongolia's 150–200 centres), field sales and referrals beat digital marketing.
5. **Network effects on supplier side:** Classplus sees value in connecting tutors (content sharing, knowledge base, tutor marketplace). This works even for small centres.

### Did NOT Transfer
1. **Pure B2C (student subscriptions):** Vedantu, Unacademy, Ruangguru all pivot to offline + hybrid because B2C subscription software is not defensible. ARPU is low, churn is high, CAC is unsustainable.
2. **Content as primary product:** Physics Wallah, Vedantu, Topica Native all sell content (video, tutoring). This works if you have a large audience or strong content advantage, but it's not what small centres want to pay for.
3. **School LMS positioning (Quipper):** Schools have different needs (IT infrastructure, integration, compliance). Don't position tutoring centre software as a school product.
4. **Freemium at scale:** Teachmint's freemium works for student acquisition but creates ARPU pressure. For sustainable revenue, you need committed, paying customers (small centres), not free users (students).

## Final Lesson: The Classplus Paradox

Classplus is the **closest analogue to Mongolia's opportunity**, but it also **cautionary tale:**

**What worked:**
- White-label app under centre's name: Small centres love it
- Payment collection: Essential, high switching cost
- Admin tools: Mandatory for scale
- Subscription + commission: Revenue alignment
- Direct sales: Efficient in small-N market (India tier II/III has ~3,000 centres; reached 100k over time)

**What almost failed:**
- Software alone (SaaS subscription) was not enough to sustain growth
- Classplus's own revenue (₹26 Cr in FY22) couldn't support the losses (₹164 Cr)
- Had to pivot to test prep (Testbook acquisition) to find a sustainable, high-demand vertical

**The implication for Mongolia:**
If you build Classplus-like software for Mongolian centres, you need either:
1. **Enough centres (critical mass):** Mongolia's 150–200 centres might be too small for pure SaaS model. Need to aggregate demand through partnerships (e.g., large tutoring chains, government education ministry)
2. **Or a complementary revenue stream:** Not just software, but also test prep (exam coaching), teacher training, or content distribution to increase lifetime value per centre
3. **Or lower cost structure:** Operate lean (1–2 founders, hire only when revenue supports it), bootstrap longer, be patient

Classplus succeeded because:
- India's market was large enough (1,100+ cities, 100k+ centres)
- Testbook gave it a second growth engine
- It raised enough capital to survive until product-market fit stabilized

**Mongolia would need to be more capital-efficient or find a complementary market (tutor training, specific exam prep, teacher certification).**

## 10-Line Summary

**Classplus's actual model:** Subscription ($240–600/year) + 15% commission on student fees. Small centres pay ~20–25% of annual revenue to Classplus when both fees are combined. **White-label won:** Each centre gets a branded app under its own name on Google Play; this high switching cost and pride-of-ownership drove adoption better than shared platforms (Teachmint, Quipper). **Centres paid for admin tools, not content:** Attendance tracking, parent messaging, payment collection, student performance dashboards—these are mandatory; content is centre-specific. **Sustainable software pricing:** $20–30/month subscriptions are realistic for centres with $5,000+/year revenue; below that, commission-based models are required. **Transferable lesson:** Direct relationship sales targeting ~150–200 known tutoring centres is more efficient than digital marketing; white-label positioning (centre's own branded app) creates stickiness; payment processing + admin tools are defensible products; but software alone is not sufficient—centres need either scale (critical mass) or a complementary revenue stream (test prep, training, content). **The cautionary note:** Classplus nearly failed with pure SaaS subscription and had to pivot to test prep (Testbook) to become viable. Mongolia's tutoring centre market (~150–200 centres) is smaller than India's, so bootstrapped capital-efficiency or a complementary vertical (exam prep, teacher certification) is essential.
